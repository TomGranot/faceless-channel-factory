## Brief

Find repeatable faceless short-video channels that reuse the strongest parts of GitTrend and Developer Launch Daily:

- a stable feed or API for discovery;
- a durable source ID and canonical source page;
- a real page, object, map, diff, or visualization that remains on screen;
- a 30 to 45 second narrated inspection with speech-aligned captions;
- enough inventory for three posts per day;
- an auditable rights and attribution path;

## Convergence

### 1. Collection Detail Reveal

This is the best first pilot. The Met, Smithsonian, Wikimedia Commons, and Library of Congress already offer large visual inventories, durable IDs, rich metadata, and workable rights filters. The guessing loop creates a reason to keep watching, and the raw material suits a phone screen better than most filings or developer pages.

### 2. Package Signal

This is the closest commercial extension of GitTrend. It serves a valuable developer audience, supports sponsors and tool partnerships, and can compound into a proprietary adoption dataset. It requires stronger anomaly checks because downloads can reflect bots, mirrors, automated builds, or one large internal deployment.

### 3. Map Patch Notes ★

This is the most distinctive viable idea. The before-and-after change is native to short video, every episode has a durable changeset ID, and the format can develop recurring local editions. The hard problem is editorial verification: a map edit can be vandalism, a mistake, or a mechanical import rather than a real change in the world.

### 4. Patent Puzzle

This has a large inventory, strong visual hooks, and a natural reveal. It can combine recent publications with evergreen historical patents. Scripts must consistently distinguish an applicant's claim from an examiner's decision, a working product, or commercial success.

## Focus branches

### Collection Detail Reveal

Query only objects with durable IDs, complete metadata, a clear commercial-use rights state, and images at least 2,000 pixels wide. Score candidates for an unusual high-detail crop, recognizable silhouette, surprising use, and four factual clues. From 0 to 6 seconds, show the crop and ask what it belongs to. From 6 to 19 seconds, reveal material, date, and place while inspecting the real collection page. From 19 to 29 seconds, pull back to the complete object and explain its original use. End on the source title, collection, and attribution.

- **Biggest risk:** automatic crops may be sharp but meaningless.
- **First test:** query 50 public-domain Met objects, render three candidates, and check on a phone whether each crop invites a plausible guess within eight seconds.
- **Children:** Guess the Material; Silhouette Only; Decode the Symbol; Ancient or Modern; One Object Across Five Cultures.

### Package Signal

Start with a bounded npm universe and reject tiny-number spikes. A first breakout score can use 50% seven-day download-growth z-score against the prior eight weeks, 25% dependent growth, 15% release-cadence change, and 10% maintainer or repository activity. Require at least 10,000 baseline weekly downloads, a durable package ID, a reachable source page, and a detected license. Open directly on the real package page and state the signal within three seconds. Scroll the description and primary example, show a compact current-versus-baseline chart, then inspect the release or repository activity that may explain the change. Close on one concrete use case.

- **Biggest risk:** automated traffic can look like genuine adoption.
- **First test:** backfill daily downloads for 50 active npm packages, score them, and inspect whether three of the top five have both a credible explanation and enough visual material.
- **Children:** Package Comeback; Replacement Wave; Tiny Tool, Huge Reach; Release Aftershock.

### Map Patch Notes

Ingest OpenStreetMap changesets and reconstruct modified geometries by durable changeset ID. A first story score can weight meaningful road, building, trail, transit, or boundary tags at 35%; visible before-and-after difference at 30%; a specific human comment at 20%; and local significance at 15%. Exclude automated imports, bulk edits, disputes, sensitive locations, and changes too small for a phone. Open on the before map, ask what is missing, and reveal the after map with the changed geometry highlighted. Zoom into the edit while explaining the changed tags and the mapper's stated reason. Finish on the real changeset with required OpenStreetMap attribution.

- **Biggest risk:** a map edit may be wrong or unverified.
- **First test:** fetch 100 recent non-bot changesets inside Amsterdam, score them, and verify whether five create clear visual differences with defensible explanations.
- **Children:** New Path Found; City Correction; Transit Map Patch; The Building Appeared.

## Traps

- A fresh source item is not automatically a worthwhile story.
- A public page does not automatically grant off-platform reuse rights.
- Public records often produce poor video unless the source includes a map, image, diagram, diff, or readable data view.
- Random selection creates repetitive scripts unless the format has a question, tension, comparison, or reveal.
- Download, job, filing, and map signals are evidence to interpret carefully. They are not proof of adoption, intent, safety, or real-world change.
- Launching several accounts at once can fragment the learning loop. Validate ten publishable items before creating a channel account.

## Recommended sequence

1. Render three Collection Detail Reveal dry runs from Met objects.
2. Run the Package Signal backfill and inspect the top five anomalies.
3. Run the Amsterdam Map Patch Notes extraction and verification test.
4. Compare source-pass rate, render-pass rate, human review minutes, and expected viewer retention.
5. Give a dedicated account only to a concept that produces ten publishable videos without lowering the rights, evidence, or visual-quality gates.
